I had been working for Wizards of the Coast for quite some time before I was shown _Magic: The Gathering_. It was the secret project they didn’t want to show to “the new guy” until I had proved myself. When I finally saw the game and had a chance to play it in its early mock-up stage I was…what would the right word be here? Amazed? Flabbergasted? In Awe? Shocked? All of those words work but still do not get across my feelings. One must remember that I was and am still a gamer. I am the target market for this sort of game and nothing like it existed in the world. I knew it was going to be big. I knew it was going to change gaming.

The original plan, before I came onboard, was to use secondary rights artwork. That is, to use artwork that had already been done for other products: think old book covers—work that would not be expensive to license. I thought this was a mistake. Why take a game as original as _Magic_ and saddle it with work done for other projects? The game deserved its own look and that was what I set out to do.

I wanted fresh artists who had had very little exposure in gaming. I wanted to create a new pantheon of respected fantasy artists, and _Magic_ would be their playground. It’s important to remember that I have massive respect for the artists that came before, but they had already set the look for their properties. Take Dungeons & Dragons for instance: there were artists working on that game who I admire and respect deeply, but they already had their place, they had already created the look of a brand. I wanted new. It should also be said that the amount we had to pay at the time would never attract these artists to begin with so the issue was rather self-solving. It was fresh blood or nothing.

I approached fellow students at Cornish College of the Arts in Seattle. I approached some of the faculty. I asked for people to recommend talented people they knew. I asked other art directors like Richard Thomas of White Wolf for recommendations. We had a very small budget, a very tight schedule, and the optimism that comes from people who have not tried and failed before.

It simply did not occur to me that what I was doing wouldn’t work. The game Richard designed was that strong. Remarkably, I had very few people turn me down. I have since been told that my passion for the project was contagious.

I tried to give the artists as much freedom as possible. My thinking was that we were creating an entire world, and a world is a diverse place. I did not want to hamstring anyone’s imagination by imposing my own over theirs. I gave as little information as I could, basically the card’s color so they had a suggested palette to work in based on the color of the card’s border and the card stats. That way the artist knew if it was a powerful card or not. Then they were on their own. The work that slowly started coming in was jaw-dropping. Every day felt like a celebratory holiday as I opened piece after piece. This was before the internet was really a thing so the originals were all mailed to me. I would scan the artwork and put it into one of the borders I had designed for the game. It was working\!

One of the rules I did enforce while art directing was that the art needed to be focused. This was not a book cover or a movie poster. This was a simple idea that needed to be clearly stated. I wanted people walking by a game of magic to be able to look down and see the subject of the cards at a glance. I did not want the playfield to look like a muddy and confusing smudge of over-detailed work. This was not the medium for that kind of artistic storytelling. Clarity was important. This philosophy went right down to designing the borders. I added texture to the colors so that colorblind people could still tell what school of magic the card belonged to. The mana circle on the back of the card was there so players could tell at a glance which colors of magic were opposed or tertiary. There was decoration on the cards but all of it was subservient to clarity.

I knew _Magic: The Gathering_ had staying power, and I am not at all surprised that it’s still around today. I may be disappointed at how corporate and “safe” it has become, but that is no comment on Richard’s original design. While the new sets may be optimized for shareholders, Old School _Magic_ is still one of the best games the world has ever seen and I expect it, in some form, to long outlive me and all of the original designers, perhaps by centuries or millennia. It is as important a game as chess, backgammon, or poker. Whatever struggles may befall this game in the years to come, it will come back. _Magic_ is forever. It’s a genie that has been let out of its bottle and it will refuse to be put back in.

Working on _Magic_ in the early days was a once in a lifetime opportunity and I am humbled and very thankful to have been a part of it. Most of all I am thankful for all of the artists who took a chance on this game and all of the players who have kept it alive.
